[ 03 / 09 ]How I work

It's not guesswork.

(My method is as simple as 1… 2… 3…)

01

One spec

Every design starts from mathematical specification and architectural constraints. In the 4-Channel DMA controller, every arbitration priority and burst boundary (1/4/8/16-word) is formalized before RTL implementation.

02

Two models

Independent software golden references and synthesizable hardware. In the Gain-Scheduled ADPLL and 2D PE Array, RTL outputs are bit-exact verified against cycle-accurate Python and behavioral models.

03

Three checks

Directed corner-case tests, constrained-random verification, and physical sign-off. Testbenches check thousands of automated vectors (6,266 checks on DMA with 0 errors) before synthesis and P&R.

[ 04 / 09 ]Verification

Spec, meet silicon.

Silicon, meet spec.

A specification says what hardware should do. RTL says what it actually does. Verification is where the two are introduced, and where every disagreement is found while it is still cheap to fix.

That is the work I do: design synthesizable microarchitectures, verify them against golden references, and take them through tapeout-ready physical design flows.

[ 08 / 09 ]The bug hunt

The bug the waveforms caught.

When the 16-bit MAC processing array first ran in RTL, 124 of 144 convolution outputs disagreed with the Python golden model.

The arithmetic was right. The multiplier trees were correct. The timing was not.

The ROM reads and controller address outputs were both registered, so MAC enable had to lag the tap index by two clock cycles. I had given it one.

I tracked it down by diffing simulation waveforms against per-tap golden accumulator values, clock cycle by clock cycle.

One more delay stage, and Conv1 matched bit-for-bit. Then Conv2. Then the entire datapath: 20 of 20 test vectors bit-exact from input activations to final logits.

The recipe never changes: a golden model, a waveform trace, and the patience to read both.
